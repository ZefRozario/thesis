\section{Introduction}
The standard model (SM) of particle physics is a fundamental description of modern day particle physics. It is the cumulations of over half-century's  intellectual efforts and describes, all known, particle interaction. However, it is not without its shortfalls in order to understand these shortfalls lets us begin to describe the theory.
\\
\\
The standard model is a relativistic quantum field theory (QFT) and is made up of three generations of fundamental particles, shown in Figure \ref{fig:SM_particles}. We can describe the SM as a gauge group, meaning it is invariant under non-local (gauge) transformations and has the group structure of:
\begin{equation}
        \mathcal{G}_\text{SM} = SU(3)_C \otimes SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y.
\end{equation}

According to Noether's theorem \cite{Noether_1971}: for every symmetry there is a corresponding conserved quantity. Here, $SU(3)_C$ is the gauge group for quantum chromodynamics, where the conserved quantity is colour charge, $C$. $SU(2)_L$, encapsulates the weak interactions and its generators, here the conserved quantity is weak isospin, L. Finally we have $U(1)_Y$ which describes the electromagnetic interactions in the SM and the conserved quantity is hypercharge, Y. The combination of gauge groups describe the weak interaction and electromagnetic sector, namely: $SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y$ is known as electroweak unification \cite{EW-uni-1,EW-uni-2,EW-uni-3,EW-uni-4}. 

\section{$\mathcal{L}_{Gauge}$}
To account for these gauge symmetries in a locally invariant manner, we introduce gauge fields associated with each component of the gauge group. These gauge fields are responsible for mediating the interactions of the Standard Model and correspond to the bosonic force carriers. Each gauge group contributes its own field: 
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    SU(3) : G^{a}_{\mu}, \;  {a=1,2,3...,8,}
    \\
    SU(2)\otimes U(1): W^{J}_{\mu}, \;{J=1,2,3}; \;B_{\mu}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $G^{a}_{\mu}$ denotes the 8 independent gluon fields and $a$ the eight gluons. They are also the generators of the $SU(3)$ group. $ W^{J}_{\mu}$, where $J=1,2,3$, represents the three gauge fields. These gauge fields are of spin-1 which are said to facilitate the interactions of the SM. We can use these gauge fields to construct the Yang-Mills Lagrangian and thus capture the dynamics of these fields. The Yang-Mills Lagrangian \cite{yang-mills} takes the form:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{gauge} = -\frac{1}{4}F^{a\mu\nu}F^{a}_{\mu\nu},
\end{equation}
where $F^{a}_{\mu\nu}$ is the field strength tensor. Accepting the definitions of: $A_{\mu} = A^{a}_{\mu}T_a$ and $F_{\mu\nu}=F^{a}_{\mu\nu}T_a$, we relate the field strength tensor to the commutator of the gauge covariant derivative $D_\mu$ by:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\mu\nu} &=\frac{i}{g}[D_\mu , D_\nu],
    \\
    &=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu} - \partial_{\nu}A_{\mu} - ig [A_\mu , A_\nu],
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mu,\nu}$ are gauge fields and $g$ is the coupling strength of the interaction facilitated by the gauge group. We now introduce the gauge covariant derivative as,
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu - i g A_\mu,
\end{equation}
Where. The gauge covariant derivative, allows the gauge field to interact with matter fields in a way that respects gauge symmetry, ensuring consistent transformation properties under local gauge transformations. Both are foundational in constructing gauge-invariant Lagrangians, which are essential for describing interactions in the SM. extending this general definition of the covariant derivative to all the gauge fields from our three gauge symmetries we have the SM covariant derivative:
\begin{equation}
    D_\mu = \partial - i g_1 \frac{Y}{2}B_\mu - i g_2 \frac{\tau_j}{2} W_{\mu}^{i}
\end{equation}

% The combination of the gague groups for  electromagnetic  and weak interactions, namely,  $SU(2)_L \otimes U(1)_Y$ is known as the electroweak unification.

 \section{$\mathcal{L}_\text{Fermion}$}
 As stated earlier, the covariant derivative allows for the interactions of gauge fields and matter fields. Consider a set of matter fields, $\Psi=\{\psi\}$ that transform under a representation of the gauge groups. These represent the fermionic fields that house the quarks and leptons that fundamental matter is made out of. The SM fermionic fields can be summarised by,
\begin{equation}\label{lepsandqs}
    L_i= \begin{pmatrix} \nu_e \\ 
    e^- \end{pmatrix}_L,\; e^-_{Ri}, \; Q_i= \begin{pmatrix} u \\ d \end{pmatrix}_L, \: u_{Ri}, \: d_{Ri},\;  i=\{1,2,3\},
\end{equation}
where $i$ represents the three generations of matter. We let these fields act on the the covariant derivative such that,
\begin{equation}
     \mathcal{L}_\text{kinetic} = i \bar{\Psi}D_\mu \Psi.
\end{equation}
\section{${\mathcal{L}_{Higgs}}$}
The Higgs mechanism \cite{higgs-mech-p1,higg-mech-p2,higgs-mech-p3} was theorised to answer the simple yet puzzling question of how do elementary particles acquire their mass? This question posed particular trouble in the early days of the unification of the weak force and the electromagnetic force (the electro weak sector). As the early gauge invariant theories contained symmetry requirements that predicted the $W$ and $Z$ gauge bosons had zero mass. This, of course, was in total contradiction of experimental results where they had non-zero masses. The introduction of the Higgs mechanism solves this problem outright. The Higgs sector consists of a doublet of complex scalar fields with hypercharge $Y_\phi=+1$
\begin{equation}
    \phi= \begin{pmatrix}\phi^+ \\ \phi^0 
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the invariant scalar potential is:
\begin{equation}
    V(\phi)=\mu^2 \phi^\dagger\phi + \lambda(\phi^\dagger\phi)^2.
\end{equation}
Solving for the minimum of $V$, we see that
\begin{equation}
    V'(|\phi|)^2=0 \Rightarrow |\phi|^2 =-\frac{\mu^2}{2\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Let us now consider the quartic coupling to be positive ($\lambda>0$) and the mass term squared to be negative ($\mu^2<0$). In this case we see that the fields develops a non-zero vacuum expectation value (VEV).
\begin{equation*}
    \phi \rightarrow \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ v+h \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation}
    \nu=\sqrt{\frac{-\mu^2}{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2}G_F)^{\frac{1}{2}}}= 246 \text{GeV},
\end{equation}
where $G_F$ is the Fermi constant and the remaining degree of freedom, $h$, is the Higgs boson. We see from the Weinberg-Salam model\cite{weinbergsalam} that the VEV is directly related to the masses of the $W$ and $Z$ bosons: 
\begin{equation}
    M_W=\frac{1}{2} \nu g, \; M_Z=\frac{1}{2}\nu \sqrt{g^2+g'^2},
\end{equation}
where $g$ and $g'$ are the $SU(2)_L$ and $U(1)_Y$ coupling constants, respectively. These are all related by the electroweak mixing angle $\theta_w$:
\begin{equation}
    \sin^2(\theta_w)=\frac{g^2}{g^2+g'^2}=1-\frac{M_W^2}{M_Z^2}.
\end{equation}
It is worth mentioning that in order to generate these masses the electroweak symmetry spontaneously breaks:
\begin{equation*}
    SU(2)_L \times U(1)_Y \Rightarrow U(1)_{EM}.
\end{equation*}
The Higgs mechanism is also responsible for generating the masses of most\footnote{The only elementary particle masses that aren't provided by the Higgs mechanism are neutrino masses. this is because in order to generate a mass term we need left and right handed chirality of the particle, neutrinos are only observed to be left handed and therefore don't generate a mass term via Yukawa interaction.} of the elementary particles we will see this in the following section.
\section{${\mathcal{L}_{Yukawa}}$}
Yukawa interactions\cite{Yukawa:1935xg} describe the interaction between  fermions and a scalar field. Adapting this to the idea of the SM we can generate mass terms for fermions (with both left and right handed chirality) as they interact with the Higgs field (a scalar field) via Yukawa interactions. Let us now consider the full unabridged Yukawa lagrangian for the Higgs field and first generation fermions:  
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{Yukawa} =-\lambda_e \bar{L}\phi e_R -\lambda_d \bar{Q}\phi d_R -\lambda_u \bar{Q} \tilde{\phi} u_R + h.c.
\end{equation}

Where $\bar{L}$ and $\bar{Q}$ are the dirac ajoin of the left handed lepton and quark doublets from Equation \ref{lepsandqs}, respectively, and defined as  $\bar{L}=L^\dagger \gamma^0$ and $\bar{Q}=Q^\dagger \gamma^0$ and $\tilde{\phi}$ is the conjugate Higgs doublet. The Higgs effective lagrangian will now contain the fermionic mass terms. Thus, the full effective lagrangian is:
\begin{equation}\label{efflag}
    \mathcal{L}_{Higgs}= g_{hWW}hW^{+}_\mu W^{-\mu} + g_{hZZ}hZ_\mu Z^{\mu} + \sum_f g_{hff}h\bar{f}_L f_R.
\end{equation}
The Higgs interaction strength increases with the mass of the particle its interacting with. Thus, the couplings to the gauge bosons and fermions\footnote{The masses of the second and third generation of fermions are generated in the same way.} can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    g_{hff}=\frac{m_f}{\nu},\; g_{hWW}=\frac{2M_W^2}{\nu}, \; g_{hZZ}=\frac{M_Z^2}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
\section{$\mathcal{L}_{SM}$}
Now that we have the full description of all the various sectors of the SM, we can put them together to for a, mostly, complete picture of the fundamental particle of the universe. Lets now summarise this picture,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{SM} &=\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{L}_{Gauge}} + \textcolor{ForestGreen}{\mathcal{L}_{Fermions}}+
    \textcolor{orange}{\mathcal{L}_{Higgs}}+
    \textcolor{blue}{\mathcal{L}_{Yukawa}}
    \\
    &= \textcolor{red}{-\frac{1}{4} G_{ \mu\nu}^a G^{a\mu\nu}}+\textcolor{ForestGreen}{i \bar{\Psi}D_\mu \Psi}+\textcolor{orange}{|D_\mu \phi|^2 -V(\phi)}+\textcolor{blue}{(\psi_i y_{ij}\psi_j\phi+h.c)}.\\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
the above equation manages to summarise the: interactions of the gauge bosons with themselves, the interactions of the Higgs field with itself, the gauge bosons and fermions and finally the fermions with gauge bosons. The interactions that include gauge bosons are described as the forces of the SM. The interactions with the Higgs field result in these particles acquiring masses post EWSB. In the SM we have three generations of matter, as to why only three or exactly three we are yet to understand. Each generation of matter consists of a pair of quarks, a lepton and a left handed neutrino, this is summarised in Fig.\ref{fig:SM_particles}.

\subsection{Higgs-top Yukawa coupling, $y_t$}\label{sec::higgs_yt}
as the top quark is the heaviest of all the know fundamental particles, Measuring the strength of its Yukawa coupling,$y_t$, is of some particular interest to us as particle physicists as it may shed some light on the hierarchy problem. Let us now briefly discuss the hierarchy problem.
\\
\\
We define the measured mass of the Higgs boson as:
\begin{equation}
    m^{2}_{H,obs}=\tilde{m}^{2}_{H,bare}+ \Delta m^2_H,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{m}^{2}_{H,bare}$ encapsulates the mass contributions of the theory parameters and $\Delta m^2_H$ are the quantum corrections from loop diagrams as shown in figure \ref{fig:top-loop}.

The top quark contributions of the quantum corrections to the Higgs mass are:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta m^2_H \approx - \frac{|y_t|^2}{16\pi^2}\Lambda^2,
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda$ is the energy scale at which new physics (NP) sits at. If we set $\Lambda \sim 10^19$GeV (the Planck scale) we see that in order for $ m^{2}_{H,obs}=125$GeV we must fine tune $\tilde{m}^{2}_{H,bare}$ in order to cancel out the large quantum corrections. This coincidence is called the naturalness problem \cite{hierachy-prob}. We see that $y_t$ plays a key role in this conundrum, as the top quark is the heaviest know particle its contribution would be the dominant factor in the value of $\Delta m^2_H$. So a precise measurement of $y_t$ would help estimate the scale of NP and where potential beyond the standard model (BSM) effects could take place. For instance, if we find large large values of $y_t$, this would imply larger corrections to the Higgs mass potential requiring NP close to the weak scale ($\mathcal{O}(100)$GeV). Alternatively, for smaller values of $y_t$ we could reduce the amount of fine tuning required which would help solve the naturalness problem.
\subsection{$t\bar{t}H$}
the $t\bar{t}H$ process, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:tth},  plays a special role in the SM as it allows for direct tree-level measurement of the top quark Yukawa coupling, $y_t$, whose importance we have laid out in Section \ref{sec::higgs_yt}. We see that from Figure \ref{fig:production_cross_sectio} that the $t\bar{t}H$ process is the least favoured production mode and so we expect the rate at which we observe the the $t\bar{t}H$ process to be much lower than all the other leading production modes. Even with this limitation, the $t\bar{t}H$ production was observed by ATLAS \cite{tth-atlas-2018} and CMS\cite{tth-cms-2018}in 2018 by the combining various decay channels. As we see from Figure \ref{fig:branching_ratio}, $H \rightarrow b\bar{b}$ is the dominant decay mode of the Higgs. So it stand to reason, that its most likely that if a $t\bar{t}H$ event occurred, the Higgs boson would most likely decay into a $b\bar{b}$ pair and so in order to have a statistical advantage in observing $t\bar{t}H$ one would only require to look here \footnote{The best laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley, / An' lea'e us nought but grief an' pain}. As simple as this plan is, it is fraught with difficulty, namely $H\rightarrow b\bar{b}$ does not have a clear signal to trigger on and so makes signal extraction, from its almost identical background, extremely difficult. There have been a few attempts at a $t\bar{t}H(b\bar{b})$ analysis in the past by both ATLAS \cite{tth-atlas-2018} and CMS \cite{tth-cms-2019} these analyses were later combined to reach the 5$\sigma$ threshold for discovery, these were based on partial Run-2 data sets. Later, ATLAS had released a full Run-2 analysis \cite{ATLAS:2022tthbb} however there has since been a reinterpretation of the full Run-2 ATLAS data set, a so-called $t\bar{t}H(b\bar{b})$ legacy analysis. This is where most of my contributions lie for the duration of my PhD and I lay these out in Section \ref{chap::analysis}.

\section{Beyond the Standard Model: Effective Field Theories}\label{sec:SMEFT}
As we begin our journey in physics, we are told that: Isaac Newton \textit{discovered} gravity or Christiaan Huygens \textit{discovered} that light is a wave. Regarding the idea of discovery with the same verve as that of a curios child spotting insects under a rock for the first time. Later in our journey, once we are shown vector calculus for possibly the third time in what seems to be unrelated university modules, it clicks: We as physicists we only describe the world around us and hope to do it in a tidy enough package that our work can be considered fundamental. Returning to Issac and his apple, a correct statement would be: Newtons laws of motion and universal law of gravity correctly \textit{describes} bodies in motion. We know that, although brilliant, Newtons works doesn’t describe everything, such as: Black-holes, Gravitational lensing, the behaviour of fast moving objects, the list goes on. The solution to all of this is of course the work of Einstein and his general theory of relativity. This of course doesn’t make Newtons laws obsolete, they still \textit{effectively} describe the motion of bodies up to a certain point. These two theories are a good example of how we can use different dynamics for different energy scales, we can also recover newtons laws (the effective theory) form general relativity (the full theory) by removing heavy degrees of freedom while still \textit{effectively} describing the dynamics at play (provided you're not moving any where near the speed of light).
\\
\\
I raise the points above as we can define the SM as an effective theory which works at a lower energy scale than the a full theory which sits at a higher energy scale. I refer of course to the Standard Model Effective Field Theory\footnote{There are of course other effective field theories that are floating around in modern day particle physics such as Heavy Quark Effective Field Theory \cite{HQEFT} designed to limit the dynamic of heavy quark interactions, for the scope of this work we will focus on the SMEFT.} (SMEFT) \cite{SMEFT} where we can extend the SM with higher dimension operators constructed from SM parameters, $\mathcal{O}$ and Wilson coefficients, $c_i$ that modify the contributions of the operators. Allowing us to quantify the possible effects of new physics that sit at a higher energy scale, $\Lambda$. The SMEFT allows for a model independent approach, we can define the SMEFT lagrangain as:
\begin{equation}\label{lag-smeft}
    \mathcal{L}_{SMEFT}=\mathcal{L}_{SM}+ \sum \frac{c_i}{\Lambda^2}\mathcal{O}^{d=6}_i +\frac{c_i}{\Lambda^2}\mathcal{O}^{d=8}_i \ldots
\end{equation}
Where the scale of the new physics suppress the BSM effects. As nice as a lagrangian is, its not much use when it come to physical observables. Luckily, we can express the SMEFT in terms of physical observables, for now let us consider cross-section, $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}\label{xs-smeft}
    \sigma_{SMEFT} = \sigma_{SM}+\frac{c_i}{\Lambda^2}\sigma_i^{\text{interf.}}+\frac{c^2_i}{\Lambda^4}\sigma_i^{\text{quad.}}.
\end{equation}
Where $\sigma_i^{\text{interf.}}$ is the interference term between the SM and the BSM, and $\sigma_i^{\text{quad.}}$ is a term that is purely dependent on BSM effects. Using Equation \ref{xs-smeft} we can see how powerful a reinterpretation of particle physics analysis' using this frame work could be, allowing us to gain hints of potential BSM effects in our SM measurements, as opposed to feeling around in the dark or lifting up a rock and surprised to not find any insects.
